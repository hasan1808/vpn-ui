PRO-UI / navigation / five schemes

Every sub-category under its section

Five ways to replace the tab strip on Settings, Xray and Core. Each one is live: click it. The content is the real inventory, twenty sub-categories across seven sections, with the real labels from translate.en_US.toml.

00

What is on the page today

Two levels of navigation that do not know about each other

Settings, Xray and Core each open with a row of seven a-tabs. Inside every tab sits a second navigation layer, an a-collapse whose panels are the actual sub-categories. Nothing outside the open tab is visible, and nothing inside a closed collapse is visible either.

The real inventory, counted from the templates:

PageSection (tab)Sub-categories (collapse panels)

Four properties of that inventory decide every scheme below.

  • The depth is uneven. General carries six sub-categories, SSL Certificate and Advanced carry none at all. A scheme that always draws two levels will look broken on half the sections.
  • One Save governs all seven sections. The button at the top of settings.html posts the whole AllSetting blob. Wandering between sections with edits pending is therefore safe, but only if the user can see that a section they are no longer looking at is holding an unsaved change. Every scheme below carries that as an amber pip.
  • Section five is conditional. Subscription Formats is rendered only when subJsonEnable || subClashEnable. Any nav has to survive a section appearing and disappearing while the page is open.
  • The tab row already overflows. Seven labels in en_US are tight; in ru_RU and fa_IR they wrap or clip. This is the concrete failure that starts the redesign.
The trap to avoid. The panel already has a nav column, the 124px .bo-rail. Adding a second permanent column inside the page means two vertical navs side by side, which is what makes most settings screens feel like a filing cabinet. Schemes 01, 02 and 04 pay that cost knowingly. Scheme 03 refuses to pay it.
01

Tree

The proposal, built properly

Sections stack down the left of the page. The open one unfolds and lists its sub-categories underneath it, indented, on a hairline. One section open at a time.

Clicking a sub-category does not swap the pane, it scrolls to that block inside the section. All of a section's fields stay in one scrollable document, so related settings remain adjacent and one Save still covers everything you can see. Sections with no sub-categories (SSL, Advanced) show no chevron and simply navigate, which is how the uneven depth stops mattering.

Try: open Telegram Bot pick a sub-category note General keeps its amber pip while you are away

What it costs

  • Front-end only
  • One new component, roughly the shape of aSidebar.html, plus a scroll-spy binding.
  • a-tabs comes out of settings.html, xray.html, core.html. The tab bodies are already separate templates, so the templates themselves do not move.
  • The collapse panels stay as they are. They become scroll anchors instead of the only way in.

What it breaks

  • Two vertical navs at once on wide screens, the rail and the tree.
  • Under about 900px the tree has to become a drawer, so mobile gets a second nav gesture.
  • Everything in a section must render at once for scroll-to to work, so the six panels of General mount together rather than on demand.
  • Indentation and the chevron both mirror in RTL. Cheap, but easy to forget.
02

Column

No expanding at all, everything is one click

The same left column, but nothing folds. Every sub-category in the whole page is listed at all times, grouped under a section heading that is a label, not a button.

The pane on the right shows exactly one sub-category. This is the macOS System Settings model. Its advantage is that the whole surface area of the page is legible without any interaction: you can see that LDAP exists without knowing it lives under General. Its cost is length. Settings has twenty sub-categories plus seven headings, which is about 750px of list against a 500px viewport, so the column scrolls while the pane does not.

Try: scroll the left column every destination on the page is in it

What it costs

  • Front-end, plus a save-model decision
  • Same component as scheme 01 with the folding removed, so it is marginally simpler to build.
  • The panes get small enough that per-sub-category Save becomes tempting. That is a backend change, AllSetting posts whole today.

What it breaks

  • A section with no sub-categories (SSL, Service, Advanced, all four Xray tables) becomes a lone entry under its own heading, which reads oddly.
  • Related fields end up in different panes. Sub certificates and panel certificates are the same job in two places already; this makes that further apart, not closer.
  • The list is long enough that its own scroll position needs remembering between visits.
03

Rail

One tree for the whole panel, no second column

There is no page-level nav, because the rail becomes the page-level nav. Settings, Xray and Core expand in place inside the existing sidebar and list their sections and sub-categories there.

This is the only scheme that removes a navigation layer instead of rearranging one. Dashboard, Inbounds and Clients keep behaving exactly as they do now; the three configuration pages simply grow a subtree. The content area gets the full width back, which matters most on Xray, where Outbounds and Routing are wide tables. The rail widens from 124px to about 232px while a subtree is open and returns when it closes.

Try: click Xray, then Settings the rail is the only nav on screen

What it costs

  • Front-end, plus routing
  • The rail is currently a flat list of anchors. It gains a subtree, an open state and a width that changes, so aSidebar.html is largely rewritten.
  • The subtree must be built server-side from the same permission gates the rail already uses, or a reseller sees section names they cannot open.
  • Deep links need to work: /panel/settings#telegram/proxy has to open the right section on a cold load, which is new behaviour.

What it breaks

  • The rail's collapsed mode. Today collapsing hides labels; with a subtree there is nothing sensible to collapse to except a flyout, which is a third interaction to design.
  • Navigating between pages currently reloads. Expanding Settings from the Xray page has to either navigate first and then expand, or the panel becomes an SPA, which it is not.
  • Nine top-level entries plus a twenty-entry subtree is a tall rail on a 720px laptop screen.
04

Scroll

Delete the tabs, keep an index

No sections in the interaction sense at all. The page is one document, every section and every sub-category is a heading in it, and a sticky index on the right tracks where you are.

The index is navigation and progress indicator at once, and the page gains something no other scheme has: the browser's own find works across the entire settings surface. Searching for "fragment" or "LDAP" with Ctrl+F simply finds it. That is a real answer to "I know the setting exists, I do not know which tab it is under", which is the actual complaint behind most settings redesigns.

Try: scroll the document the index follows or click an index entry

What it costs

  • Front-end, plus a render-cost problem
  • Simplest markup of the five: headings and an IntersectionObserver.
  • No new component if the index is a plain sticky list.

What it breaks

  • Everything mounts at once. Today a-tabs keeps six of seven bodies unmounted; here the SSL manager, the Telegram form and the LDAP block all initialise on load. Xray is worse: Outbounds and Routing are full tables.
  • Scroll position and the amber pip fight each other. A dirty field three thousand pixels up is out of sight in a way tabs never allowed.
  • Xray Advanced is a full-height JSON editor. It cannot live in a scrolling document without trapping the wheel.
05

Strip

Keep the tabs, add the missing row and a search

The conservative option. The tab row stays and gains horizontal scroll so it stops clipping in ru_RU and fa_IR. A second row of pills below it exposes the sub-categories of whichever tab is open. Ctrl+K searches every individual field, not just category names.

Two rows of chrome is more than the other schemes spend, and the pill row is empty on the sections that have no sub-categories, which is visible dead space. In exchange this is the only scheme that keeps a-tabs and its lazy mounting, so it is the one that can ship in an afternoon and cannot regress page load.

Try: the tab row scrolls sideways pick a pill click the search box and type "frag", "ldap" or "port"

What it costs

  • Smallest change of the five
  • a-tabs keeps its structure. The pill row is one loop over the collapse panels of the active tab.
  • The palette needs a flat index of every field label, which is a build-time list, but it also needs those labels translated in all thirteen locale files.

What it breaks

  • It does not fix the thing you asked to fix. Sub-categories are still hidden until you pick the section, one level at a time.
  • Horizontally scrolling tabs hide their own overflow. Seven tabs where two are off-screen is the same discoverability problem in a different shape.
  • Two stacked rows eat 84px of vertical space on every configuration page.

Side by side

Counted against the real inventory, not a hypothetical one
Scheme Clicks to any sub-category Visible without interacting Navs on screen Lazy mount kept Cost

02 Column 1 All 27 rows, by scrolling 2 Per sub-category Front-end + save model

05 Strip 2 7 sections, minus overflow 1 (two rows) Per section, unchanged Smallest

The recommendation is 01, shaped so it can become 03 later. Tree is what you described, it survives the uneven depth without special cases, and it keeps one Save honest by keeping a section's fields in one scroll. If the tree is built as a standalone component that takes a section list and emits a selection, rather than as markup baked into settings.html, then moving it into aSidebar.html later is a re-parenting rather than a redesign, and scheme 03 stops being a rewrite.

Two things are worth taking from the other sheets regardless of which is chosen. The amber pip from every scheme here is not decoration: with one Save covering seven sections, it is the only way a user can tell that leaving a section did not lose their edit. And the Ctrl+K palette from 05 is orthogonal to all of this, so it can ship on its own, before or after.

Not yet decided by any of these. Whether Core joins Settings and Xray under one nav, or stays its own page. Core is the only one of the three whose tabs are a workflow rather than a set of forms, and the schemes above all assume forms.
