Five ways to show an inbound

The same four inbounds, five information architectures. Today one 799-line template renders an inbound and an account through the same two-column table of purple tags; these five are answers to the inbound half only, and to the two questions an operator actually opens it with: how is this configured, and why can a client not connect.

Everything is drawn in the panel's own tokens, so what you are choosing between is the structure, not the paint. Protocol chips stay neutral; hue means state.

Inbound
Optimised for
Diagnosis. Why a client cannot connect.
The change
The fields are ordered by where they sit on the connection, and a stage that cannot pass traffic severs the chain below it.
Costs
It is a narrative, so it is tall. Fields with no place on the path (quota, sort order, traffic multiplier) are pushed to a tab this mockup does not draw.
Long values
Truncated on one line, full value in the tooltip and on the copy button. Fastest to scan, worst to read.
Optimised for
Looking one thing up. Every field, in a fixed place.
The change
Six groups by concern with the real field name in the key column, so what you read here matches what you search the codebase for.
Costs
Nothing leads. It is the longest of the five and it will not tell you anything is wrong.
Long values
Wrapped, never hidden. Rows become uneven, which is the price of a reference view that does not lie by omission.
Optimised for
The reason you opened it. Something is broken.
The change
One sentence on whether it can serve, the findings that justify it, and the configuration folded behind four disclosures.
Costs
When nothing is wrong you get a green box and a click before you can read anything. It also cannot tell "does not apply" from "not shown", because inapplicable fields never surface at all.
Long values
Truncated inside the folds. Findings quote the field name, never the value, so a key never has to fit.
Optimised for
Giving it to someone else. An outbound, a router, a customer.
The change
The modal produces artifacts instead of listing fields. Recipients on the left, the finished block on the right, copy in one action.
Costs
It shows no usage and no health at all, and every family needs a hand-written template. A new protocol lands here as an empty pane until someone writes one.
Long values
Wrapped inside a code block at full length, the pane scrolling past 340px. A truncated config is a broken config.
Optimised for
Is it full. Bytes, quota, accounts, addresses.
The change
Capacity is the headline and configuration is a strip at the bottom. The address pool is drawn as discrete slots, because its unit is "one more account".
Costs
A vless or vmess inbound has no address pool, so on the protocols this panel is most used for a third of the layout has nothing to draw and says so.
Long values
Barely arises: this view shows figures, not keys. Range strings clip.

Sample data only. Four inbounds chosen to cover what the real modal has to survive: an Xray-native one with reality (a 43-character public key and two short ids), a VPN-family one with no stream settings at all (address ranges, DNS pair, MTU, IPsec PSK, user limit and strategy), one that is genuinely broken (TLS enabled with a blank certificate file, and a 63-character websocket path), and one with no accounts and no port at all.

Field names are the real ones, from database/model/model.go, web/assets/js/model/inbound.js and the protocol forms under web/html/form/protocol. Nothing here is a runtime probe: every value shown is something the panel already stores, except where a panel is labelled as proposing something new.
